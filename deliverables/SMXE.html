Who should be on social media?

This deliverable involved writing a social media bot for Bluesky using the Authenticated Transfer Protocol (ATP or atproto). ATP is a decentralised social media protocol and allows publishing and distribution of data on social media platforms.

The bot I wrote can be found here. At the most basic level (literally 2 lines of python), the bot can make a text post. My favourite kinds of posts online are vague, meme-y posts, and so I tried emulating that for my test text posts.

The second level of functionality (still just a few lines of code) allows the bot to post images. Keeping with the theme of vague meme image, I made my bot post an outdated meme.

Finally, the most exciting and bot-like functionality that my bot has is the reply function. When my bot is mentioned (@'ed) in a post or reply, it will reply to that with information on Elon Musk's wealth, specifically, how far he is from a net worth of $1 trillion. The bot uses the yfinance package, that allows scraping information from Yahoo! Finance. This combines both the user interaction metric and the external package metric into a cohesive function of the bot.

For the basic text and image functionalities of the bot, atproto's python library provides very convenient, syntax-sugared functions. Once the bot is declared an instance of the Client class and is logged in, the code for making a post is as simple as saying

    
        def make_text_post(text):
            client.send_post(text)

Social media sometimes feels like a black hole. It claims (and I think sometimes achieves) to have a wide variety of applications for human-related uses, such as staying connected or raising awareness or sharing art or crowdfunding. However, recently, it feels more like a black hole, where the platform's goal is not to provide a good, fulfilling user experience, but rather to optimise for watch times and ad revenue.

It also feels like a difficult area of human life to govern. On one hand, there are ideas of freedom of speech and lack of censorship; the idea that the internet should be completely free and there should be no sort of censhorship. On the other hand, the anonymity granted by the internet allows people to voice a lot of harmful opinions that they otherwise maybe would not express. Finding the balance and the correct ethic here is very difficult.

Particularly, this comes up in the case of bots. 3 of our top 5 class questions dealt with bots, their governance and their existence. Specifically, a question that still lives in my mind is the one asking whether it is okay for a bot to harm some people if it makes more people happy?

I think the reason this question intrigues me is because societally, we have already answered it. Societally, we have already accepted bots that harm some people if it makes other people happy. This is seen in ad bots and scam bots that aim to profit off other users, usually real people. Societally, we have accepted this as a real possibility that people face on social media: it's an occupational hazard.

I also think the more nuanced view on this is to realise why this is true. I think the reason is because bots allow companies to 'farm' on social media, be it engagement or click-rate or ad revenue. The corporate gamification of social media through bots is what makes it impossible for society to say no: the stakeholders are too happy about the bots because they make it easier to make money.

All this is not to say that social media is irrevocably ruined. There are still good, humanitarian and socially beneficial bots, such as the one we read about in the Botivist reading. However, it is important to recognise that the negative reinforcement and effect of the bad bots is much more harmful and more widespread than the good done by the good bots.
